# Power Play: Keep the Final Say Over Your AI

**Three quick wins:**

- **Rules for what AI can touch:** A one-page draft of rules for what your team may share with, and connect to, AI tools, ready for you to edit.
- **A plan before files move:** A written plan for tidying your file storage or inbox that you approve before anything is moved.
- **Make your proposal sound like you:** The passages in your proposal that read as machine-written, each rewritten in your voice, facts unchanged.

---

## #1: Rules for what AI can touch

*A one-page draft of rules for what your team may share with, and connect to, AI tools, ready for you to edit.*

**TL;DR:** Paste the tools and accounts your team uses and the AI tools they use, then answer a short list of questions. You get a rule sheet sorting each item into fine to share, connect with a person checking, keep out, or decide later, with a reason each.

**Why it matters:** Once anyone on your team is trying AI tools, the question of what they may paste in or connect is already live. One owner's assistant, unable to reach a calendar invitation, instead messaged everyone on it through Slack; nobody had chosen that. A simple test came out of that: give a tool access only where you would accept it making changes on its own, never where you would have to undo a mistake. Written down, your team can follow it.

**Before you start:**

- A list of the tools and accounts your business runs on (email, calendar, chat, accounting, customer records, file storage, and so on).
- The AI tools your team uses or is considering.
- Optional: any rules you already have, even informal ones.

**Copy this prompt:**

```text
My small team uses AI tools and I want a short written rule sheet on what they may share with, and connect to, those tools. Pasted below are the tools and accounts we use, the AI tools we use or are considering, and any rules we already have. If they are not pasted, ask me for them first.

First, ask me in one short list only what you need: for each account or type of information, whether I would be fine with an AI tool making changes there on its own or would have to undo a mistake; which information is confidential to clients or covered by contract or law; and anything I have already decided. Wait for my answers.

Then draft a one-page rule sheet with four groups: 'Fine to share', 'Connect only with a person checking each action', 'Keep out of AI tools', and 'Decide later'. Put every item I listed in exactly one group with a one-line reason, using my answers exactly as given. Apply this sorting rule and tell me it is a suggestion I can change: anything I would have to undo does not go in 'Fine to share'. Anything I don't know goes in 'Decide later' with the question I need to answer. Label the sheet a proposal for me to edit.
```

**How to tell it worked:** Every account and type of information you listed appears in the sheet exactly once, and nothing you said you would have to undo sits under 'Fine to share'. Items you were unsure about appear under 'Decide later' with a question, not a placement. If an item is missing, doubled, or placed against your answer, paste it with your answer and ask it to place it once and say why.

## #2: A plan before files move

*A written plan for tidying your file storage or inbox that you approve before anything is moved.*

**TL;DR:** Paste the names of your current folders or email labels and a few lines on how you look for things. You get a proposed structure, a table placing every existing folder once, open questions, and a small first batch to check, with nothing moved.

**Why it matters:** Asking an AI to organize your files directly invites it to guess what you want and act on the guess. One owner spent months on it: the tool scrambled her Drive, then reported it could not move things. The fix is to separate thinking from doing: ask for a plan, edit it until it matches how your team works, and only then let anything move. A big cleanup is exactly where that pause pays off.

**Before you start:**

- The names of your top-level folders (or your email labels), copied from your account.
- A few sentences on who uses these files and what you look for most often.
- Optional: anything that must stay exactly where it is.

**Copy this prompt:**

```text
I want to reorganize my files, but nothing should move yet. Pasted below are the names of my current folders (or email labels) and notes on how my team looks for things. If the names are not pasted, ask me for them first. Do not move, rename, delete, or change anything; produce only a plan.

First, ask me in one short list anything you still need: who uses these files, what I look for most often, and anything that must stay where it is. Wait for my answers.

Then write a plan under one page with four parts: (1) a proposed folder structure with a one-line purpose per folder, presented as a suggestion I can change; (2) a table listing every current folder exactly once with where it would go, as 'keep', 'move to ...', or 'merge into ...', with a one-line reason; (3) anything you are unsure about, as questions for me rather than guesses; (4) the order of steps, starting with a small first batch I can check before the rest. Do not propose deleting anything. If a folder seems unneeded, list it under 'For me to decide' with what it seems to be for and what might depend on it.
```

**How to tell it worked:** Every folder name you pasted appears in the table exactly once as keep, move, or merge. Nothing is marked for deletion; anything questionable sits under 'For me to decide' with its purpose noted. The step order starts with a small batch. If a folder is missing or appears twice, paste its name and ask it to add it to the table once with a reason.

## #3: Make your proposal sound like you

*The passages in your proposal that read as machine-written, each rewritten in your voice, facts unchanged.*

**TL;DR:** Paste your proposal, and if you have one, a sample of your own writing. You get each passage that reads as machine-written, why it does, and a rewrite that keeps every price, date, and commitment as written.

**Why it matters:** A coach had a client ready to sign, sent a proposal she had drafted with AI, and the client balked because it seemed AI-written, though the content was hers. The advice: have the AI read your document as someone who spots machine-written patterns and point to exactly where they show, so you fix those lines yourself. No rewrite can promise how a reader will react; you get the lines to make your own before sending.

**Before you start:**

- Your proposal as text you can paste.
- Optional: a sample of something you wrote yourself, such as an email or a web page, so the rewrites match your voice.
- Optional: any feedback the client gave you.

**Copy this prompt:**

```text
Below is a proposal I plan to send to a client, and, if I included one, a sample of my own writing. If the proposal is not pasted, ask me for it first.

Read the proposal as an editor who knows the patterns that make text read as machine-written. List every passage that shows such a pattern. For each: quote it exactly as it appears, say in one line why it reads that way, and give a rewrite in my voice, matching my sample if I gave one. Keep every fact, price, date, name, and commitment exactly as written; change wording only, and add no new claims or promises. Do not give a score, rating, or verdict on whether the document passes as human-written; I only want the passages and the rewrites.

End with up to three questions where a specific detail from my own experience with this client would make a passage more personal, so I can fill them in myself. Keep the whole reply under 600 words.
```

**How to tell it worked:** Every quoted passage appears word for word in your proposal, and each rewrite keeps the same prices, dates, names, and deliverables. There is no score or pass/fail verdict, only passages and rewrites. If a rewrite changes a fact or a quoted passage is not in your document, paste that passage and ask it to redo only that one with the facts unchanged.

---

## The through-line

Each of these puts your decision in front of the AI's action: rules before your team connects anything, a plan before a single file moves, and your own voice before a proposal goes out. The tools are fast and eager; these three habits keep you the one who decides.
